California's replacement for net metering spent four years in court. That fight ended on June 10, 2026, when the California Supreme Court declined to hear a further appeal, closing off the last route by which the rules might have been sent back for another rewrite. For anyone deciding today whether solar still makes sense for a district's pumps and wells, the practical point is simple: exported power is worth a small fraction of what it paid under net metering, that is not going to change in a courtroom, and a system sized to sell power back to the grid will not produce the savings the old arithmetic promised. A system sized to use its own power, with a battery doing the shifting, still can.

## Overview of the Net Billing Tariff

The California Public Utilities Commission adopted the Net Billing Tariff in Decision 22-12-056 on December 15, 2022. That is the tariff's formal name; the Commission's own materials do not use the nickname "NEM 3.0" that press and industry coverage settled on, though the two terms describe the same thing ([CPUC, Net Billing Tariff](https://www.cpuc.ca.gov/industries-and-topics/electrical-energy/demand-side-management/customer-generation/nem-revisit/net-billing-tariff)). The tariff applies to any customer who submitted an interconnection application on or after April 15, 2023. Anyone who applied before that date keeps their net energy metering terms, NEM 1.0 or NEM 2.0, for 20 years from the date their system was interconnected ([NRG Clean Power](https://nrgcleanpower.com/learning-center/nem-3-0-is-final/)). The Commission adjusted the tariff with what it called minor modifications in Resolution E-5301 on November 30, 2023 (CPUC, cited above).

## Exported power does not pay

Under NEM 2.0, a kilowatt-hour sent to the grid was credited at close to the retail rate the customer would otherwise have paid to buy it, commonly 30 cents or more. The Net Billing Tariff pays something different: the hourly value the grid places on that power at the moment it is exported, calculated through the Commission's Avoided Cost Calculator. That number is far smaller than the retail rate it replaced. Based on the 2024 update to the calculator, average export credits for recent applicants run about 3.9 cents per kilowatt-hour at PG&E, 4.6 cents at SCE, and 3.7 cents at SDG&E ([Energy Toolbase](https://energytoolbase.com/?p=1331), as summarized by NRG Clean Power, cited above). The retail rates those same utilities charge for power a customer buys back run roughly ten times higher: PG&E's average bundled residential rate was 39.25 cents per kilowatt-hour in March 2026, and SCE's was 35.3 cents in January 2026 (NRG Clean Power, cited above). Export rates are also locked to a vintage: a system's compensation is generally fixed for a period tied to the year it was interconnected, about nine years under current guidance, so a system interconnected next year will likely be locked to a different rate than one interconnected this year (NRG Clean Power, cited above). A district's own rate schedule for an agricultural or industrial account will carry different numbers than a residential one in both directions, and should be checked rather than assumed. The shape of the gap between buying and selling a kilowatt-hour does not change by customer class.

## The legal fight

The Center for Biological Diversity challenged the Net Billing Tariff, arguing it would slow rooftop solar growth and ignore the benefits of local generation, particularly for disadvantaged communities. An earlier Court of Appeal ruling upheld the tariff under a deferential standard of review. The California Supreme Court disagreed with that approach: in a ruling issued August 7, 2025 (Center for Biological Diversity, Inc. v. Public Utilities Commission, 18 Cal. 5th 293 (2025), docket S283614), it held that the Court of Appeal should have applied the stricter "Yamaha" standard to the Commission's statutory interpretation, and sent the case back for another look ([Climate Case Chart](https://www.climatecasechart.com/document/center-for-biological-diversity-v-public-utilities-commission-of-the-state-of-california_98ac)). Applying that stricter standard on remand, the Court of Appeal again upheld the tariff on March 9, 2026, finding the Commission had acted within its authority and reasonably balanced costs and benefits across customer classes (Center for Biological Diversity, Inc. v. Public Utilities Commission, No. A167721, 2026 WL 657656 (Cal. Ct. App. Mar. 9, 2026), as summarized by [Stoel Rives](https://www.lawofrenewableenergy.com/2026/03/articles/states/california/the-california-court-of-appeal-rules-in-favor-of-the-cpucs-net-billing-tariff/)). The California Supreme Court's June 10, 2026 refusal to hear a further appeal closed off the remaining path to a different outcome ([PV Magazine USA](https://pv-magazine-usa.com/2026/06/10/california-supreme-court-declines-to-hear-rooftop-solar-billing-case/)). Changing the compensation formula from here would require the Commission to revisit it on its own, or the Legislature to act. The plaintiffs have said they intend to keep pushing on that policy track, which is a slower and less certain route than a court order, and nothing currently pending commits either body to take it up.

## Battery addition to the rescue

The dollar value of a kilowatt-hour your own meter never sees leave the property is the full retail rate you would otherwise have paid for it. The dollar value of a kilowatt-hour you export is the few cents described above. A battery's job is to convert more of the second kind into the first, by holding midday solar output for the afternoon and evening hours when a district's pumps are still running and the utility would otherwise be billing at a demand charge or a peak time-of-use rate. One illustration, built around a hypothetical SCE residential account using 10,000 kilowatt-hours a year with half self-consumed and half exported: solar alone saves roughly $1,995 a year; adding a battery that shifts 3,000 of the exported kilowatt-hours into evening use adds roughly $920 more, for about $2,915 a year (NRG Clean Power, cited above). That is one utility's residential illustration, not a water district's well field, and it says nothing about what a battery costs to buy or finance. A battery is a cost before it is a saving, and whether it pencils for a particular system depends on load shape, the size of the system relative to onsite demand, and the rate schedule in force, not on the figures above.

## What this changes

A district that already has solar interconnected under NEM 2.0 terms is not affected by any of this until that 20-year term runs out. It is new systems, and any expansion of an existing system that counts as a new interconnection, that fall under the Net Billing Tariff. Before assuming either tariff applies, check the interconnection application date on file, not the date the system was first discussed or financed. None of this tells a district whether its particular pumps, its particular rate schedule, and a particular battery quote add up. That is a reason to run the specific numbers against the district's own load data, not a reason to treat solar as settled either way.
